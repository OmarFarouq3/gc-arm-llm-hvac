# Expert rules on chiller-plant energy efficiency

- Low chilled-water delta T ("low delta T syndrome"): supply and return temperatures too close means more water is pumped per unit of cooling, extra chillers or pumps run at low load, and heat exchange is insufficient. Common causes are excessive flow, bypass or decoupler flow, open coil valves, or a wrong temperature sensor reading; it is usually an operational anomaly.
- High cooling-tower approach at high fan speed (leaving water far above wet-bulb although the fan is fast) indicates reduced heat-transfer capability of the tower, such as fouled fill or scaling. This raises condenser water temperature and chiller power and is a performance degradation.
- Rising chiller power, condenser approach or condenser water temperature at the same load and weather over time indicates heat-exchanger fouling (condenser or evaporator) and lower COP: performance degradation.
- Low chiller or plant COP at medium or high load, when outdoor conditions are mild, suggests degraded equipment or poor operation; low COP at very low part load can be normal because of fixed losses.
- A temperature sensor with a constant offset (bias) or drift makes any control loop that uses it hold the wrong real temperature and waste energy. Readings that disagree with neighbouring sensors, with energy balances, or with their set-point while the controller reports it is satisfied point to a sensor fault: operational anomaly.
- A pressure or flow sensor that reads wrong (offset, drift, blocked tap) makes pumps or valves controlled from it run too fast or too slow, wasting pump energy or starving equipment of flow. Pump or valve output that does not match the load points to a sensor or control fault: operational anomaly.
- A three-way valve that leaks or is stuck sends condenser water around the towers when cooling is needed (or through them when it is not), giving condenser temperatures that do not follow the wet-bulb set-point: operational anomaly.
- Poorly tuned PI control loops show up as oscillating fan speed, valve position or temperature around the set-point, frequent cycling and extra energy use: operational anomaly.
- Equipment that is persistently oversized or undersized for the load (chillers always at very low part load, pumps always at minimum speed, towers unable to meet design approach on design days) points to a design anomaly rather than an operating fault.
- Cooling-tower fan power rises roughly with the cube of fan speed; condenser water about 1 degF warmer typically costs a few percent more chiller power, so tower and condenser problems have a large energy impact.
- Refrigerant undercharge (leak): low evaporator pressure and a large evaporator approach (chilled-water leaving temperature well above refrigerant temperature), the chiller cannot reach full capacity on hot days, and COP falls: performance degradation.
- Non-condensable gases (air) in the condenser: condenser pressure and condenser approach rise while condenser water temperatures are normal, so compressor power rises at the same load: performance degradation.
- Clogged strainers or worn pump impellers: flow falls at the same pump speed or more speed and power are needed for the same flow, and pressure differences across the pump drop: performance degradation.
- Leaking or always-open AHU cooling-coil valves: chilled water passes through coils without picking up heat, return water is too cold, delta T is low and pumps run faster than the building load requires: operational anomaly.
- A chiller isolation valve stuck open on an idle chiller lets chilled water bypass the running chillers, mixing warm water into the supply; supply temperature rises above set-point and more chillers or pump speed are called for: operational anomaly.
- Wrong chiller staging: more chillers running than the load needs (each at very low part load and poor COP) or too few (supply temperature cannot be held), or rapid start/stop cycling: operational anomaly.
- Chilled-water or condenser set-point not reset with outdoor conditions (stuck at the design value all year): the plant works harder than needed in mild weather, so chiller power is high for the load and COP is low in cool seasons: operational anomaly.
- Failed cooling-tower fan or motor (fan command on but no airflow or near-zero fan power): that tower's leaving water stays close to its entering water, condenser water warms and the other towers or chillers work harder: operational anomaly.
- Chiller running against a high condenser water temperature at low load because the tower set-point is fixed too high wastes compressor energy; lowering it towards wet-bulb plus approach improves COP: operational anomaly.
