# Expert rules on chiller-plant energy efficiency

- Low chilled-water delta T ("low delta T syndrome"): supply and return temperatures too close means more water is pumped per unit of cooling, extra chillers or pumps run at low load, and heat exchange is insufficient. Common causes are excessive flow, bypass or decoupler flow, open coil valves, or a wrong temperature sensor reading; it is usually an operational anomaly.
- High cooling-tower approach at high fan speed (leaving water far above wet-bulb although the fan is fast) indicates reduced heat-transfer capability of the tower, such as fouled fill or scaling. This raises condenser water temperature and chiller power and is a performance degradation.
- Rising chiller power, condenser approach or condenser water temperature at the same load and weather over time indicates heat-exchanger fouling (condenser or evaporator) and lower COP: performance degradation.
- Low chiller or plant COP at medium or high load, when outdoor conditions are mild, suggests degraded equipment or poor operation; low COP at very low part load can be normal because of fixed losses.
- A temperature sensor with a constant offset (bias) makes the controller hold the wrong real temperature: e.g. a leaving chilled-water sensor reading too high makes the chiller over-cool and use more power. Readings inconsistent with neighbouring sensors (supply vs. loop temperature, set-point vs. measured) point to a sensor fault: operational anomaly.
- A differential-pressure sensor that reads low makes the secondary pumps speed up and waste pump energy; reading high starves coils of flow. Pump speed that does not match the load, or pumps near full speed at low load, indicates an operational anomaly.
- A three-way valve that leaks or is stuck sends condenser water around the towers when cooling is needed (or through them when it is not), giving condenser temperatures that do not follow the wet-bulb set-point: operational anomaly.
- Poorly tuned PI control loops show up as oscillating fan speed, valve position or temperature around the set-point, frequent cycling and extra energy use: operational anomaly.
- Equipment that is persistently oversized or undersized for the load (chillers always at very low part load, pumps always at minimum speed, towers unable to meet design approach on design days) points to a design anomaly rather than an operating fault.
- Cooling-tower fan power rises roughly with the cube of fan speed; condenser water about 1 degF warmer typically costs a few percent more chiller power, so tower and condenser problems have a large energy impact.
